# Clinical sufficiency rubric

This rubric asks whether a resident can reason about the discharge medication plan. It does not require a longer chart for its own sake. A fact is added only when the discharge decision depends on it and the existing chart does not already supply it.

The question is: does the chart contain the clinically relevant information necessary to reason about the discharge medication decisions?

When the decision depends on them, the chart should let a reader see:

- the initial symptoms;
- the baseline against which an acute change is judged;
- a disease precipitant, or an explicit statement that none was found;
- the relevant work-up rather than every possible test;
- important diagnostic results that were obtained;
- the treatment that was given;
- the response to that treatment;
- medication changes during the hospitalization;
- renal function and electrolytes when a medicine depends on them;
- hemodynamics when volume or perfusion is part of the decision;
- bleeding or infection status when an anticoagulant, antiplatelet, or antimicrobial is at issue;
- discharge stability;
- monitoring and follow-up.

Different syndromes need different information. The lists below are internal revision guides. They are not fields that must all be populated.

## Delirium

Potentially necessary: baseline cognition; an acute change from that baseline; a likely precipitant; the work-up that makes that precipitant interpretable; treatment of the precipitant; the cognitive response; and renal or metabolic data when a medicine depends on them.

An example such as urinary infection is not a required diagnosis. Another coherent precipitant can meet the rubric.

## Heart failure

Potentially necessary: evidence of congestion; a relevant precipitant evaluation, which may be adherence, arrhythmia, ischemia, infection, dietary or volume factors, or another clinically relevant trigger; inpatient treatment; response to treatment; weight or fluid trajectory when it exists; renal and electrolyte trends; hemodynamic stability; and cardiac imaging only when it is already recorded or is deliberately created with provenance. Discharge medication constraints stay tied to the recorded doses. An intravenous loop-diuretic dose is not invented merely because many hospitalizations use one.

## Endocarditis

Potentially necessary: the presenting syndrome; culture information; organism information only when it is genuinely available; source or risk context; echocardiography; complications that bear on an operation; renal function; the antibiotic that was given; duration only when a duration is supported; whether surgical evaluation is relevant; and follow-up. Fever, a dental extraction, a prosthetic valve, and a species name are not added unless a separate provenance decision records them. This revision does not add those four items.

## Cytomegalovirus and transplantation

Potentially necessary: symptoms before the diagnosis; transplant history; the immunosuppressive drugs that are actually on the source list; the test that establishes cytomegalovirus; renal function; electrolytes when they bear on the decision; blood counts when the antiviral decision needs them; antiviral timing; renal-dose context; and transplant or infectious-diseases follow-up. A drug that is absent from the source list, including mycophenolate when it is absent, is not added to make the regimen look typical.

## Gastrointestinal bleeding and anticoagulation

Potentially necessary: how the bleeding appeared; the hemoglobin trend; source or control of bleeding when that information is represented; renal function; the indication for anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy; the thrombotic context; hemodynamic stability; and the timing or follow-up that bears on a restart. Absence of endoscopy is stated when no endoscopy exists. It is not filled in with an invented lesion.

## Atrial fibrillation and warfarin

Potentially necessary: the indication; the INR; bleeding status; an interacting treatment when one is relevant; renal or hepatic context when the decision uses it; and monitoring.

Passing this rubric means the case is detailed enough to review. It does not mean a clinician has accepted the case.
